Pump not running and water coming up: what to do right now

Stay out of any standing water if a panel, outlet or appliance is wet or low to the floor. Water and live electricity kill people, so do not wade in to check the pump.

Then call (773) 207-0518. While you wait, use the steps below to slow things down.

  • Shut off power to the wet area at the breaker only if you can reach the panel on dry ground.
  • Stop running the washer, dishwasher and showers, because their water often ends up in the same pit or line.
  • Move boxes, rugs and stored items off the floor and onto shelves if it is safe to do so.
  • Look at the pump from a dry spot. If the float is jammed against the pit wall, tell us when you call.
  • Do not pour bleach or drain cleaner into the pit.

Sump pump, ejector pump or check valve: which one is yours

Callers often say sump pump when they mean a different device. The name matters because the repair, the part and the risk are different.

Three pump-related devices found in older Blue Island basements
DeviceWhat it movesTypical failure
Sump pumpGroundwater and rain seepage from a pit in the floor, out through a discharge pipeStuck float, burnt motor, clogged intake, power loss
Ejector pumpToilet, sink and laundry wastewater from a basement bathroom up to the main drainWorn seals, jammed solids, failed alarm or switch
Check valveNothing, it only stops pumped water from falling back into the pitStuck open or cracked, so the pump re-pumps the same water
Battery backupSame job as the sump pump, but on a deep-cycle batteryDead battery, never tested, undersized for a long storm

If your basement bathroom has a sealed lid in the floor, you likely have an ejector pit. That is sewage, so treat any overflow as contaminated and keep children and pets away.

Why heavy storms hit Blue Island basements so hard

Most of Blue Island is served by a combined sewer, which carries household sewage and storm runoff in the same pipes. The Cook County hazard mitigation plan blames widespread urban flooding on the age and capacity of that system, and names low spots such as Washington Street and Vermont Street near California. If your home sits in one of those dips, a pump that fails mid-storm has little time to spare.

A nonprofit study counted 469 flood-related insurance claims in ZIP 60406 between 2007 and 2011, with more than $1,279,191 paid. That is why we ask about your discharge line and check valve on every visit, not only the pump itself. A new pump that pushes water into an overloaded line only buys a few minutes.

Installing a new pump or ejector pit is plumbing installation, which Blue Island code section 150.052 says needs a permit. The Illinois Plumbing Code (77 Ill. Adm. Code 890) is the state code. Discharge rules vary by municipality, so ask Building Safety where your pump may drain before running a line to the curb.

How a pump replacement goes, start to finish

Most replacements take part of a visit once we have the right pump on the truck.

01

Test and diagnose

We lift the float by hand, check power at the outlet, and run water into the pit to see whether the pump starts and the water leaves.

02

Pull the old pump

We disconnect the discharge union, lift the unit and clear silt, gravel and debris from the pit bottom.

03

Set the new pump

The new pump goes on a level base with a fresh check valve and a union, so the next service is quick.

04

Test under load

We pour in enough water to lift the float, then watch a full cycle and listen for chattering that signals a bad check valve.

05

Show you the controls

You see the breaker, the outlet and the float, and we explain what the test button or alarm on your backup does.

Backup choices for when the power goes out

Storms that flood basements often knock out power too. A pump with no backup is a pump that stops at the worst moment.

Battery backup pump

A second, smaller pump sits beside the main one and runs on a battery. It buys hours, not days, so the battery must be tested.

Pump with built-in alarm

A high-water alarm cannot stop a flood, but it gives you a warning while you are still home and awake.

Second primary pump

Two pumps in one pit, staged at different float heights, cover a pump failure and an unusually heavy inflow.

Check valve upgrade

A clean check valve on the discharge keeps pumped water from draining back into the pit. This is the cheapest weak link to fix.

Warning signs before the pump fails

Pumps rarely die without hints. If you see any of these, call before the next forecast.

  • 1It runs constantly. A pump that never shuts off may have a stuck float or an undersized motor.
  • 2It makes grinding or rattling sounds. Worn bearings or a loose impeller will fail soon.
  • 3It cycles on and off every few seconds. That short-cycling usually means a failed check valve or a bad float switch.
  • 4The pit has rust, silt or oily film. Debris shortens pump life and can jam the float.
  • 5The pump is older than you can remember. If nobody knows when it was installed, plan a replacement before it chooses the date.
