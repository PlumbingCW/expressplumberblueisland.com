What you notice first, and what it usually means

Most callers describe a symptom, not a part. That is fine. Match what you see to the closest line below and we will confirm the real cause when we arrive.

Water coming up through a floor drain.

Basement drains lifting with every rain or flush often point to a sewer line problem. See sewer line cleaning and keep the washer off.

A ceiling stain that keeps growing.

A soft, darkening patch under a bathroom or kitchen means water is still arriving. Leak detection finds the source without opening half the wall.

No hot water, or lukewarm at best.

A tank that runs cold, rumbles or drips at the base is dying. Start with water heater repair, and decide on replacement only if the tank itself is leaking.

Every drain in the house is slow.

When the tub, sink and toilet all gurgle together, the blockage is deeper than a trap. Hydro jetting clears the main line.

Low pressure on the whole street side of the house.

A sudden drop at every tap can mean a broken service line. Call about water line repair and check the meter for movement.

A toilet that will not stop running or keeps overflowing.

Close the valve behind the bowl first. Many fixes are quick, see toilet repair.

Why Blue Island basements back up in heavy rain

The City of Blue Island says most of the city is served by a combined sewer system, which carries household sewage and storm runoff in the same pipes. In a hard downpour those pipes fill, and the water has nowhere to go but back toward the lowest drain it can find. The Calumet-Sag Channel runs through town, and the city lists overflow points into it at Irving Avenue and Division Street.

For you, that means a floor drain in the basement is the first place to watch. The nonprofit Center for Neighborhood Technology counted 469 flood-related insurance claims in ZIP 60406 between 2007 and 2011, and it recommends check valves and overhead sewers for homes that keep flooding. If your drain gurgles during storms, call us for an assessment, and ask your insurer what your policy covers.

Cold snaps, old pipes and the 36-inch rule

Chicago-area (O'Hare) climate normals put the January average low near 18.8 F, with 38.4 inches of snow in a typical year. Pipes in exterior walls, unheated garages and crawl spaces take the hit when a cold snap arrives. If a faucet runs slow in January, shut the line and call before it splits.

The Illinois Plumbing Code (77 Ill. Adm. Code 890) says a water service pipe must be buried at least 36 inches deep or below local frost depth, whichever is deeper. That matters if you are planning a new service line or an addition. Call 811 before anyone digs, since JULIE needs at least 2 working days to mark buried utilities.

The Illinois Plumbing Code (77 Ill. Adm. Code 890) is the code the city of Blue Island lists among those it follows. Plumbing permits go through Building Safety under section 150.052 of the city code. You can check any plumber's licence on the state lookup before work begins.

Water from Chicago, and what to ask about your service line

Blue Island buys its water from the City of Chicago, which draws from Lake Michigan. The city's Water and Sewer Department reported an inventory on April 1, 2025 of 5,433 service lines, with 529 confirmed lead and 4,707 still unknown. If you are replacing a main shutoff or a water line, ask the department what material your service line is made of.
