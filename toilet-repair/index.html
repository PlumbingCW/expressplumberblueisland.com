Most toilet problems come from four small parts: the flapper, the fill valve, the wax seal and the flange. Each one fails in its own way, and each one has a different fix. Knowing which one you have saves a visit that ends with the wrong part in the truck.

Toilet overflowing or leaking right now? Do these things first

Stay calm and stop the water before you reach for a plunger. Lift the tank lid, push the flapper down onto its seat, and turn the small oval valve on the wall or floor behind the bowl clockwise until it stops. That shutoff valve cuts the supply to this toilet only, so the rest of the house keeps running water.

  • Close the valve behind the toilet. If it will not turn, shut the main water valve where the line enters the house, often in a basement or utility closet.
  • Do not flush again, and do not run the sink, tub or washer on the same bathroom line until a plumber looks at it.
  • Keep everyone away from wet floors near outlets, power strips and bathroom heaters. Switch off the breaker if water has reached an outlet.
  • Lay towels around the base and move rugs and cabinet contents out of the wet zone.
  • If water is also bubbling from a floor drain or tub, the problem is deeper than the toilet. Say so when you call (773) 207-0518.

Overnight and weekend calls get the same priority as weekday ones. Our emergency line is open around the clock for overflows and leaks that are damaging ceilings below.

Signs your toilet needs a plumber, not another flush

Callers describe the same handful of symptoms over and over. Match yours to the list, then tell the dispatcher what you see.

  • 1The bowl gurgles after a flush. Air is being pulled through a partly blocked line, so check whether the tub or a nearby sink also drains slowly.
  • 2Water pools around the base. A failed wax seal or cracked flange lets water out every time you flush, and it soaks the subfloor.
  • 3The toilet rocks or shifts. Loose closet bolts or a flange that has pulled away from the floor will break the seal again if you only tighten the bolts.
  • 4The tank refills for no reason. A worn flapper leaks into the bowl, and the fill valve restarts every few minutes.
  • 5Two toilets will not flush. When more than one fixture backs up, the blockage is usually in the branch or main drain, so see sewer line cleaning.

Repair the toilet or replace it? A quick decision table

A toilet is a long-lived fixture, so repair is often the right call. Replacement makes sense when the porcelain itself is failing or when repeat repairs cost more than a new unit.

How a toilet repair visit works

We keep the process simple so you know what is happening in your bathroom.

01

Find the source

The plumber checks the tank parts, the supply line, the shutoff valve and the base before touching anything, because a base leak can start at any of the three.

02

Pull the toilet if needed

For seal and flange work, the bowl comes off the floor, the old wax is scraped clean and the flange is inspected for cracks, rust and height.

03

Repair or replace the failed part

That may be a flange repair ring, a new seal, a fill valve, a flapper or a stainless supply line, set with new closet bolts.

04

Reset and test

The bowl is set level, bolted evenly, flushed several times and checked for weeping around the base and at every connection.

You get the price before the work starts. If the floor under a failed seal is damaged, we show you and explain what that changes.

Why older Blue Island bathrooms have flange trouble

About 64.7% of Blue Island housing was built before 1970, and the median home dates to 1961. Bathrooms that old have often been re-floored at least once, and a flange can end up sitting slightly low after new flooring goes down. A flange below the finished floor is one reason a new seal never holds.

Roughly 48% of Blue Island households rent, and about 41% of homes sit in buildings with two or more units. If you rent, a leak at the base can drip into the unit below, so report it to your landlord and call us quickly so the damage stays in one apartment.

The federal standard for toilets is 1.6 gallons per flush, and EPA WaterSense models use 1.28 or less. If an old toilet needs replacing rather than repair, a WaterSense model can cut water use. Plumbing work for hire in Illinois falls under the Illinois Plumbing License Law (225 ILCS 320).

New toilet installation: what to settle before the visit

Swapping a toilet looks simple until the old one comes up and the flange is rotted. A little planning avoids a second trip.

Bowl shape and height

Round bowls save space in tight rooms. Elongated bowls and comfort-height seats suit most adults.

Flush type

Single-flush and dual-flush models both work. Look for a WaterSense label if you want a water-saving model.

Old fittings

We check the flange, the shutoff valve and the supply line and replace whatever looks tired while the bowl is off.

Old toilet removal

Tell us if you want the old bowl hauled away. A porcelain toilet is heavy and awkward for one person to carry down a stairwell.

What moves the price of toilet repair

A fill valve swap is a small job. A flange repair with floor work is a bigger one. We give you a clear price before work starts, and we do not guess over the phone about what is behind the wall.

Which part failedA flapper or fill valve costs less than a flange repair or a new toilet.

Fixture choiceA basic bowl and a tall, dual-flush model sit at very different price points.
Time of the callA midnight overflow costs more to staff than a scheduled daytime visit.
AccessA second-floor bathroom over a finished ceiling needs more care than a basement powder room.
