Old supply piping rarely fails all at once. It shows up as brown water in the morning, a shower that dribbles when someone runs the dishwasher, or a third pinhole leak in the same basement run. When repairs start repeating, repiping may cost less than patching again. Call (773) 207-0518 to book a walk-through, and a licensed plumber will go over what needs to change.

Below you will find the signs worth acting on, how galvanized and older copper behave, what moves the price, and the order the work follows in an occupied home.

Signs your supply pipes are nearing the end

One symptom alone rarely settles it. Two or three together usually mean the pipes, not the fixtures, are the problem.

  • 1Brown or rusty water from cold taps. It points to corrosion inside steel pipe and tends to be worst after the water sits unused.
  • 2Low pressure at several fixtures. Mineral and rust buildup narrows the pipe bore over time.
  • 3Repeat leaks. Pinholes in copper or leaks at threaded galvanized joints in different spots are a pattern, not bad luck.
  • 4Green crust or flaking on pipe. Visible corrosion on exposed copper or rust on steel.
  • 5Pipe that hums or knocks. Loose or scaled pipe may rattle with flow changes.

When to call right away about failing supply pipes

Most repipes are planned, but a few signs mean the pipe is failing now.

  • 1Water is spraying or a joint is bulging. Close the main shutoff and call (773) 207-0518.
  • 2A ceiling or wall stain is growing or dripping. Shut off the main, place a bucket, and keep clear of any wet light fixture.
  • 3Pressure drops at every fixture at once. Confirm the main shutoff is fully open, then call if it is.
  • 4Water stays brown or rusty. If it does not clear after the cold tap runs for several minutes, stop using it for drinking and call.
  • 5Water is near the electrical panel or outlets. Stay away from the area and call from another room.

Galvanized steel and older copper: why they wear out

Galvanized steel pipe is coated with zinc, and over decades that coating and the steel under it corrode from the inside. The pipe can look sound outside while its bore shrinks, which is why pressure fades before leaks appear. Joints are usually the first weak points.

Copper lasts far longer, but it is not immune. Aggressive water, flux residue and vibration can cause pinhole leaks, and older solder joints sometimes fail. If one section of copper has failed, we check the rest before recommending a partial or full repipe.

Which Blue Island homes tend to need it

About 64.7% of Blue Island housing was built before 1970, with a median build year of 1961. That is the era when galvanized supply pipe was common, so many homes here are candidates for a close look.

Single-family bungalows and cottages

About 44.3% of housing units are detached single-family homes. Basement access often makes whole-house work practical.

Two-flats and small multi-unit buildings

About 7.7% of units are in two-unit buildings and 8.2% in three or four. Risers shared by floors need coordinated shutoffs.

Apartment buildings

Units in buildings of 5 to 19 units make up roughly a quarter of the stock. Repiping usually goes in phases by riser.

Historic downtown buildings

Buildings in the Olde Western Avenue Historic District need a Certificate of Appropriateness for permitted alterations, so ask the city before opening walls.

The sequence from walk-through to final test

A repipe is a project with a plan. Here is the order it follows in a lived-in home.

01

Walk-through and scope

We trace the lines, locate the shutoffs and meter, and decide whole-house or partial.

02

Price and schedule

You receive a clear price before work starts and agree on days when water will be off.

03

Permit

Blue Island code requires a plumbing permit for installation and repair. A plumber applies as the owner's agent.

04

Rough-in and inspection

New lines go in beside the old ones. The contractor calls the Plumbing Inspector before pipes are covered.

05

Connect, test and patch

We connect fixtures, pressure test, then close access holes. Water starts after the city confirms compliance.

Phased or all at once

Most owners worry about living without water. We agree on the water-off windows before work starts, and we plan around when the household needs water.

Phasing makes sense if a tight budget, a tenant-occupied building or finished walls are in play. Do the worst runs first and plan the rest. The trade-off is that you pay for mobilization more than once.

Permits, inspections and checking your plumber

Under Blue Island code, § 150.052, a plumber applies for the plumbing permit, and the Plumbing Inspector must verify a valid plumbing license, or that the applicant is the owner-occupant of a single-family home. State law, 225 ILCS 320, requires licensed plumbers.

You can check any plumber on the Illinois Department of Public Health Plumbing License Verification page. Call the Blue Island Building Safety Department with questions before the project starts. If a leak is active while you plan, see burst pipe repair or leak detection first.

Copper water tube is made to ASTM B88, which separates Types K, L and M by wall thickness, so your quote should say which type goes in your walls. New supply piping must also meet the Illinois Plumbing Code (77 Ill. Adm. Code 890).
