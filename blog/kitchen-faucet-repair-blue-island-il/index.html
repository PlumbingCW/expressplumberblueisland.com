Kitchen Faucet Repair in Blue Island, IL: What You Can Fix and What to Hand Off

A drip at the spout, a weak stream or a wobbly handle usually has a small cause. Here is how to find it before you buy parts.

Most kitchen faucet problems in Blue Island homes come down to one worn part: a cartridge, a rubber seat, an O-ring or a clogged aerator. A steady drip from the spout means the internal seal has worn out. Weak flow usually means mineral grit in the aerator or a kinked supply line. A leak under the sink points to a loose nut or a cracked hose.

You can handle several of these in an afternoon with a basin wrench, an adjustable wrench and a hex key. Others, like a seized shutoff valve or corroded pipe behind the wall, are better left to a licensed plumber. This guide shows which is which, and what to do first so a small repair does not become a flooded cabinet.

Read the symptom before you buy a part

The way a faucet fails tells you most of what you need to know. Match what you see to the likely cause, then decide whether to open the faucet yourself.

  • 1Drip from the spout when the handle is off. The cartridge, ceramic disc or valve seat is worn. Replacing the cartridge fixes it most of the time.
  • 2Water at the base of the handle. A dried-out O-ring or loose packing nut lets water creep up the stem. This is usually a cheap fix.
  • 3Weak or sputtering stream. Unscrew the aerator and look for white or gray grit. If the aerator is clean, check the supply hoses for a kink and the angle stops for a half-closed valve.
  • 4Spray from the side of a pull-down hose. The spray head diverter or the hose connection has failed. Both are replaceable without removing the faucet.
  • 5Wobbling faucet body. The mounting nut under the sink has loosened, or the sink deck has cracked around the hole.
  • 6Wet cabinet floor. Dry the area, wipe each fitting with a paper towel and watch which one darkens first. That is your leak.

If the paper towel stays dry at every fitting but the cabinet is still damp, the water may be coming from the drain trap or the dishwasher line, not the faucet.

How a basic cartridge swap works

Most single-handle kitchen faucets use a replaceable cartridge. The job takes about 30 to 45 minutes once the water is off, and the part is often the only thing you need to buy. Take the old cartridge to the store so you match its shape and spline count.

01

Shut off the water

Close both angle stops under the sink by turning them clockwise. Open the faucet to release pressure and confirm the flow stops.

02

Plug the drain

A small screw or retaining clip can vanish down the sink. Stuff a rag in the drain or set the stopper first.

03

Remove the handle

Pry off the cap, back out the set screw with a hex key and lift the handle straight off. Take a phone photo of every layer as you go.

04

Pull the old cartridge

Unscrew the retaining nut or pull the clip, then lift the cartridge out. Wipe the brass body clean of grit.

05

Seat the new one and test

Align the notch, tighten the nut by hand plus a quarter turn, then open the stops slowly and run the faucet for a minute while you watch for seepage.

Older two-handle faucets with compression stems work differently. They use a rubber washer and a brass seat, and the seat itself can be pitted. A pitted seat needs a seat wrench or a new faucet, and that is a good point to call a plumber.

Find your shutoffs before anything drips

Every repair above starts with knowing where the water stops. The valves under the sink isolate only that faucet. The main shutoff controls the whole house, and you should be able to reach it in under a minute.

In an older Blue Island house the main valve is usually in the basement near the meter, on the wall where the service pipe enters.

Turn it a full quarter or several rotations depending on the valve type, then open a faucet at the lowest point to confirm the flow has stopped. If a main shutoff is stuck or leaks around the handle, a plumber can replace it with a ball valve that works every time.

The Illinois Plumbing License Law (225 ILCS 320) is the state's licensing rule for plumbers. Blue Island code section 150.052 lets an owner-occupant of a single-family home apply for a permit, so a DIY faucet swap in your own house is a different case from hiring someone. If you hire, check the licence.

When a repair is really a replacement

A faucet that is 15 or 20 years old may cost more to repair than to replace. Watch for these signs before you spend an afternoon on parts.

Parts are discontinued

If the manufacturer no longer sells your cartridge, a universal part often leaks again within months.

Finish is flaking or pitted

Corrosion on the body means the metal itself is failing, and no seal will fix that.

The faucet leaks in two places

Two failures at once usually mean the valve body is worn. Replacing the whole unit is cleaner.

The sink deck is cracked

A cracked mounting hole cannot hold a new faucet firmly and may need a plumber and a countertop fix.

Aerators, grit and your tap water

A clogged aerator is the cheapest fix on this page. Unscrew the tip of the spout, rinse the screen under running water and use an old toothbrush on the mesh. A vinegar soak for an hour loosens the crusty deposits that build up around the holes.

If the screen clogs again within weeks, mineral content in your water could be the cause, or loose scale from old pipes upstream. Blue Island does not publish a hardness figure in its 2025 water quality report, so a cheap test strip from a hardware store is the practical way to find out what is coming out of your tap.
