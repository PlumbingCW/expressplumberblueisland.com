Copper Plumbing Pipe Types for Blue Island, IL Homes: K, L, M and DWV

Four copper tube types look alike but do different jobs. Here is how to tell them apart and where each belongs in a house.

Copper plumbing tube comes in four types: K, L, M and DWV. Types K, L and M carry pressurized water and differ in wall thickness, with K the thickest and M the thinnest. DWV is a thin-walled tube made for drains, waste and vents, where there is no pressure to contain.

Each type is printed with a stripe of color so you can identify it at a glance. Knowing the code helps when you read a repiping quote, inspect an old basement or decide whether a section of pipe is worth saving. In Blue Island, where most homes pre-date 1970, it also helps you tell original copper from later repairs.

The four types at a glance

The Copper Development Association lists the color codes and standards below. Wall thickness runs from thick to thin in the order K, L, M, so a heavier type holds more metal between the water and the wall.

Copper tube types, color codes, standards and typical uses
TypeColor codeStandardWhere it is used
Type KGreenASTM B88Thickest wall. Underground water service, fire protection, fuel oil, solar and HVAC
Type LBlueASTM B88Medium wall. Domestic water inside the house, plus natural gas and LP gas lines where code allows
Type MRedASTM B88Thinnest of the three pressure types. Domestic water distribution, fire protection and HVAC
DWVYellowASTM B306Drain, waste and vent piping only. Not for pressurized supply

The color is the quickest check. A yellow stripe on a drain stack is expected. A yellow stripe on a pressurized supply line is a red flag that someone used the wrong tube.

Type K: the underground workhorse

Type K has the thickest wall, which suits pipe that is buried, flexed and loaded by soil. Water service lines that run from the street to the house are a classic Type K application.

The Illinois Plumbing Code (77 Ill. Adm. Code 890) calls for water service pipe to sit at least 36 inches deep or below frost penetration, whichever is deeper, so the pipe has to hold up under real weight and cold.

You will also see Type K in fire protection and snow melting systems. Most homeowners never touch it, but it matters when you hear a plumber say the service line needs replacing. The service line is the pipe between the street connection and your home, and that choice of material affects how long it lasts.

Type L and Type M inside the walls

Type L is the standard for interior water in much of the country. It handles pressure with a margin to spare and stands up to the vibration and expansion that hot water lines endure. Type L also appears on gas piping where local code permits.

Type M has a thinner wall. It works for interior distribution in many houses and costs less, but a thinner wall leaves less metal to wear before a pinhole forms. If a plumber offers both types, ask which one the local inspector prefers. Blue Island does not publish a rule on this in the sources we reviewed, so the Building Safety Department is the place to confirm what it accepts.

How to identify the copper in your basement

A short walk through the basement can tell you what you have. Bring a flashlight and a rag to wipe dust off the pipe.

  • 1Look for the printed stripe. Letters and a color stripe are stamped along the tube. Green means K, blue means L, red means M and yellow means DWV.
  • 2Check where the pipe enters. Find the main shutoff and meter. The first few feet of pipe often differ from the rest of the house.
  • 3Note the joints. Soldered joints look like small silver rings at each fitting. Compression and flare joints are a sign of later repairs.
  • 4Look for green or white crust. Crusty fittings are slowly leaking. Wipe them dry and watch for a bead of moisture over an hour.
  • 5Tap the drain stack. Larger pipes in cast iron or plastic belong to the drain system. Copper DWV is rare and usually runs in short branches.

Photograph what you find. A clear picture of the labels and the shutoff makes any plumber's estimate faster and more accurate.

What Blue Island's housing age means for your pipes

The median Blue Island home was built in 1961, and about 64.7% of the housing stock pre-dates 1970. That puts a large share of local pipework in the age range where original materials are wearing out. Many houses combine original supply lines with pieces swapped in over the decades, so you may find several metals in one basement.

Mixed metals matter. When copper meets galvanized steel without a dielectric fitting, the steel corrodes faster at the joint. A plumber can separate the metals with the right union or recommend replacing the steel section. If you see rust-colored water or low pressure at the fixtures, mention the mixed pipe to the plumber right away.

Lead service lines: a different question from copper

The copper inside your house is only part of the water path. The service line from the street can be a different material. Blue Island's inventory dated April 1, 2025 lists 5,433 service lines in total. Of those, 529 are lead, 81 are galvanized pipe requiring replacement, 116 are non-lead and 4,707 are still marked unknown.

That means an unknown line is the most common case. In August 2026 the city offered free in-home identification, a visual check of the line where it enters the home that takes about two to five minutes. If you have never had your line checked, call the Water and Sewer Department and ask whether another visit is scheduled.

How a plumber approaches a repipe

When copper has worn out, a repiping project replaces the supply lines through the house. The sequence below shows how a licensed crew usually works.

01

Survey and plan

The plumber maps every fixture, notes the pipe type and decides which runs to replace and how to reach them.

02

Permit and shutoff

Work that alters plumbing is covered by Blue Island code section 150.052, which requires a plumbing permit applied for through the Building Department.

03

Cut and fit

Old runs come out and new copper or another approved material goes in. Soldered joints are cleaned, fluxed and heated until the solder flows.

04

Pressure test and inspect

The system is filled and checked for leaks, and the plumber calls the Plumbing Inspector before pipes are covered.

05

Patch and restore

Walls and floors are closed up once the inspector signs off.
